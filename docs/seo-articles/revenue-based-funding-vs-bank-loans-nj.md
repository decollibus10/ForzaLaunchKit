# Revenue-Based Funding vs. Bank Loans for NJ Business Owners

New Jersey business owners have more than one way to look for working capital. A bank loan, a line of credit, vendor terms, equipment financing, and revenue-based funding can all solve different problems.

The important thing is not which option sounds fastest. It is whether the structure actually fits the business, the cash flow, the use of funds, and the existing obligations.

FORZA CAPITAL PARTNERS LLC is building its first pilot around business receivables purchases for eligible New Jersey businesses. This article explains how that differs from a traditional bank loan.

## What Revenue-Based Funding Means

Revenue-based funding is generally built around business receivables. In a receivables-purchase structure, the funder purchases a specified amount of future business receivables. The merchant receives an upfront advance, and remittance is tied to the purchased receivables according to the agreement.

That is different from a consumer loan and different from a traditional bank loan. The structure should be explained clearly before any business owner signs anything.

- A first funding request of $5k-$15k.
- Business-purpose use of funds.
- A receivables profile that can support the structure.

## What a Bank Loan Means

A bank loan is usually debt. The bank approves a principal amount, interest rate, repayment schedule, and maturity date. The business is expected to make payments according to that schedule regardless of whether revenue is up or down.

Bank loans can be a strong option for businesses with established credit, clean financials, strong collateral, and enough time to complete the underwriting process. A bank line of credit can also be a useful tool for businesses that qualify.

The tradeoff is that bank underwriting may be slower, more document-heavy, and less flexible for businesses with inconsistent revenue, recent growth, or urgent timing.

## Key Differences Owners Should Understand

Revenue-based funding and bank loans should not be compared only by speed. Business owners should compare the full structure.

Important differences include:

- **Underwriting focus:** Bank loans may emphasize credit, collateral, tax returns, debt service, and financial statements. Revenue-based funding usually emphasizes deposits, receivables, balances, sales consistency, existing positions, and business cash flow.
- **Payment structure:** Bank loans usually have fixed payments. Revenue-based funding should have clear remittance and reconciliation terms.
- **Pricing language:** Bank loans usually use interest rates. Merchant cash advance and receivables-purchase agreements often use factor rates.
- **Timing:** Revenue-based funding may move faster after documents are complete, but speed should not replace careful review.
- **Fit:** A bank loan may be better for long-term projects. Revenue-based funding may be better for short-term working-capital gaps when the business has receivables to support it.

## Why Factor Rates Are Different From Interest Rates

A factor rate is multiplied by the advance amount to calculate the total purchased receivables amount.

For example, a $10,000 advance at a 1.50 factor rate means the total purchased receivables amount is $15,000 before any eligible discount or adjustment described in the agreement.

That is not the same as an annual percentage rate. Business owners should ask for the total dollar amount, remittance method, fees if any, reconciliation process, and any discount terms in writing.

## When Revenue-Based Funding May Fit

Revenue-based funding may make sense when a business:

- Has recurring revenue or receivables.
- Needs a short-term working-capital bridge.
- Has a specific business use of funds.
- Can support remittance without harming payroll, rent, taxes, or vendors.
- Understands the total purchased amount and reconciliation process.

Examples may include inventory timing, receivables gaps, short-term repairs, seasonal labor needs, or marketing tied to near-term demand.

## When a Bank Loan May Be Better

A bank loan, line of credit, or another option may be better when:

- The project is long-term.
- The business qualifies for lower-cost bank capital.
- Cash flow cannot support faster remittance.
- Margins are already too thin.
- Existing advances are stacked.
- The owner does not understand the total cost or contract terms.

A responsible funding review should sometimes result in no offer. A decline can protect both the business owner and the funder.
