How Factor Rates Work in a Merchant Cash Advance

Learn how factor rates work in merchant cash advance and receivables-purchase agreements, including total purchased amount and discount examples.

If you are comparing merchant cash advance offers, one of the first terms you will see is the factor rate.

A factor rate is not the same thing as an interest rate. It is a multiplier used to calculate the total purchased receivables amount in a merchant cash advance or receivables-purchase structure.

Understanding that math before a call can help you avoid confusion and compare offers more carefully.

What Is a Factor Rate?

A factor rate is usually shown as a decimal, such as 1.20, 1.35, or 1.50. The factor rate is multiplied by the amount advanced to calculate the total purchased amount.

For example:

  • Advance amount: $10,000.
  • Factor rate: 1.50.
  • Total purchased receivables amount: $15,000.

That means the business receives $10,000 upfront and the agreement is structured around $15,000 in purchased receivables, subject to the final contract terms.

Why It Is Not the Same as Interest

Interest usually accrues over time. A factor rate usually calculates a fixed total purchased amount at the start of the transaction.

That difference matters. A factor rate does not automatically tell you the annualized cost, the remittance pressure, or how the agreement behaves if revenue changes. You need the full structure to understand the offer.

Business owners should ask:

  • What is the advance amount?
  • What is the factor rate?
  • What is the total purchased amount?
  • Are there fees?
  • How is remittance collected?
  • Is there reconciliation?
  • Is there any discount for early satisfaction?
  • What happens if revenue drops?

How the 30-Day Discount Example Works

FORZA may also explain an eligible 30-day prepayment discount example. If an agreement allows a discount to a 1.10 total amount within 30 days, the same $10,000 advance could have a discounted satisfaction amount of $11,000 if the business qualifies and follows the agreement.

Example:

ItemAmount
Advance amount$10,000
Standard total purchased amount at 1.50$15,000
Eligible 30-day discount amount at 1.10$11,000
Potential savings$4,000

The key words are eligible, example, and agreement. A discount should be written clearly and should not be treated as automatic unless the signed terms say so.

What Good Pricing Communication Looks Like

A business owner should be able to see the full dollar math before making a decision.

Clear communication should include:

  • Advance amount.
  • Factor rate.
  • Total purchased amount.
  • Any fees.
  • Remittance method.
  • Estimated timing.
  • Reconciliation terms.
  • Discount terms, if any.
  • Events that could cause breach or default.

If a funding offer only emphasizes speed and hides the total amount, slow down.

Questions to Ask Before Signing

Before signing any MCA or receivables-purchase agreement, ask:

  • Is this structured as a loan or a purchase of future receivables?
  • What exact amount am I agreeing to remit?
  • What percentage or payment schedule applies?
  • Can remittance be reconciled to actual revenue?
  • What happens if sales drop?
  • What happens if I want to satisfy the agreement early?
  • Are there other fees or deductions?
  • Is there a confession-of-judgment clause?
